01 / DIPLOMACY
138 BCE

An envoy heads west.

Emperor Wu of Han sends Zhang Qian toward Central Asia in search of allies. His difficult journeys expand the Han court’s knowledge of peoples, horses and markets to the west.

Not the beginning of all exchange—but an important new connection.
IN EXCHANGEHorses · diplomatic knowledge
02 / COMMERCE
c. 70 CE

Silk becomes a Roman obsession.

Chinese silk reaches Roman consumers through many hands. Merchants in Central and West Asia connect distant markets; very few people travel the entire distance between China and the Mediterranean.

Luxury goods could cross continents even when their buyers never met their makers.
IN EXCHANGESilk · glassware · spices
03 / RELIGION & ART
366 CE

Belief leaves its mark.

Work at the Mogao Caves near Dunhuang is traditionally dated to 366 CE. Over centuries, Buddhist communities and patrons create paintings, sculptures and manuscripts that reveal a world of cultural exchange.

Monasteries and oasis settlements offered more than a place to rest.
IN EXCHANGEBuddhist texts · artistic styles
04 / CULTURAL EXCHANGE
c. 700 CE

A city welcomes the world.

Tang-era Chang’an becomes a cosmopolitan imperial capital. Sogdian merchant communities, associated with cities such as Samarkand, help carry goods, languages, music and religious traditions across Central Asia.

Trade transformed the everyday life of the cities it passed through.
IN EXCHANGEMusic · textiles · languages
05 / TECHNOLOGY
751 CE

Paper travels. Knowledge follows.

Tang and Abbasid forces clash near the Talas River. A later tradition connects captured papermakers with Samarkand, but paper was already moving west. By the late eighth century, paper-making is established in Baghdad.

The familiar story of one battle is simpler than the real process of technological exchange.
IN EXCHANGEPaper · translation · scholarship
06 / EMPIRES & TRAVEL
1271 CE

Journeys across Mongol Eurasia.

Marco Polo leaves Venice with his father and uncle. Mongol rule connects large parts of Eurasia and, in some places and periods, supports long-distance travel. War, difficult terrain and uncertainty remain part of the journey.

Political connections could make travel possible; they did not make it easy for everyone.
IN EXCHANGETravel accounts · commercial contacts
07 / DISEASE
1340s

Connection carries a cost.

Plague moves through interconnected land and sea networks. The Black Death reaches Mediterranean ports in the 1340s, bringing devastating loss of life. Ships, trade and human mobility help disease spread.

The history of exchange includes danger as well as discovery.
IN EXCHANGEMovement of people · pathogens
08 / CHANGING NETWORKS
1400s

The journey moves out to sea.

Maritime commerce becomes increasingly important, and European voyages seek new routes to Asian markets. Trade does not disappear from the land routes: networks change at different speeds in different places.

There is no single date when all the Silk Roads “closed.”
IN EXCHANGEIndian Ocean commerce · spices
09 / HISTORICAL MEMORY
1877

A new name for an old network.

German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen popularizes the term “Silk Road,” or Seidenstraße. The name helps describe a long history of exchange—but one road cannot capture the many routes, people and centuries involved.

The people who traveled these routes did not share one name for the whole network.

03 / More than merchandise

What traveled along the way?

Most merchants traveled only part of the network. Goods and ideas passed between many different hands.

01 — GOODS

Silk, spice & precious things

Chinese silk, Central Asian horses, Indian cotton, spices, glassware and other goods moved through interconnected markets. Different regions supplied different treasures.

COMMERCE CONNECTED DISTANT MARKETS
02 — KNOWLEDGE

Ideas that crossed borders

Paper, artistic techniques, scientific knowledge and stories spread between societies. These exchanges involved translation, adaptation and new inventions.

KNOWLEDGE GREW THROUGH EXCHANGE
03 — BELIEFS

Cultures in conversation

Buddhist monks traveled from South and Central Asia toward China. Christianity, Islam and other traditions also spread, alongside languages and artistic styles.

PEOPLE CARRIED THEIR WORLDVIEWS

Connection had a cost, too.

Deserts, mountain passes, war and political change made journeys dangerous. Interconnected land and sea routes also helped diseases spread, including plague in the fourteenth century.
